# ARCANA: The Great Debate

An original turn-based strategy game for the existing 23-card ARCANA collection.

Design draft, not yet implemented or playtested. Suggested duration: 15–25 minutes. Two players, or one player against a bot. English and Romanian presentation. Romanian title: **ARCANA: Marea Dezbatere**.

## The premise

Two schools of thought enter a city whose inhabitants have lost their shared answers. You assemble a council of thinkers and contest three questions. Winning means gaining the city's attention; it does not establish philosophical truth.

The collection's portraits, foil, rarity and personas remain. Its decorative energy and attack numbers do not determine game balance. Game costs and effects are separate, visible on a switchable play face.

## First playable rules

- Three debate arenas: **Reality** (What exists?), **Consciousness** (What is a self?), and **Meaning** (How should we live?). Later scenarios can substitute God, knowledge or society.
- Each player chooses six different philosophers from all 23. Both players may choose the same philosopher. All cards are available from the start; opening packs has no competitive advantage.
- Each side has two spaces in each arena. Start with an empty board, six cards in hand, zero influence in every arena, and zero prestige.
- A match lasts eight rounds. Each round contains one turn for each player, followed by scoring. Starting player alternates each round.
- At the start of your turn, gain three **Insight**, up to five stored Insight.
- Take up to two actions. An action is: deploy a philosopher from your hand for one Insight; move one of your philosophers to an available space in another arena for one Insight; make a basic argument for one Insight; or activate a signature power for its listed cost.
- Each philosopher may act only once per turn: a move, argument or signature activation exhausts it until your next turn. Newly deployed philosophers can act immediately if an action remains. Deployment does not itself exhaust the card.
- A basic argument adds two of your influence to that philosopher's arena. Influence is a shared score for your side of that arena, not attached to individual cards. Influence ranges from zero to ten.
- Unless specified otherwise, a power affects its user's arena. No effect may make influence or Insight negative. Effects resolve immediately without reaction interrupts in the first version.
- **Guard** prevents one opposing effect from reducing your influence in its arena, then disappears. Maximum one Guard per side per arena. Replacement, swapping and setting influence count as reduction if they lower it. Paying your own influence is not prevented.
- At the end of each round, the side with more influence in each arena gains one prestige. A tie awards neither side anything. Influence remains for the next round.
- After round eight, the higher total prestige wins. If tied, higher total influence wins. If still tied, the match is a draw.
- End turn is always available. No draw pile, random damage, elimination or free-text judging is needed for this first ruleset.

The strategic choice is whether to defend a narrow lead, challenge a dominant arena, or move a thinker into a question your opponent has neglected.

## The 23 signature powers

Every card has a basic argument and the following signature. Its second illustrated collectible power is reserved for an advanced ruleset; the initial game does not pretend all 46 are already balanced abilities.

These effects are metaphors inspired by the thinkers. They do not encode historical philosophical victories.

Effects with conditions check those conditions before modifying scores. Powers with optional quantities may choose zero. A power requiring a target is unavailable if no legal target exists. Multiple uses of Schopenhauer's effect do not stack; only one extra Insight is charged. The opponent's argument count resets at the start of their turn, whether or not a Pendulum is active.

## A memorable exchange

Reality begins at Kant's side 2, Hume's side 1. Both cards are deployed and ready.

1. Kant spends two Insight on Transcendental Lens. His side now has four influence and Guard.
2. On the following turn, Hume makes a basic argument for one Insight. His side reaches three influence.
3. Hume is now exhausted, so he cannot also activate Causal Fracture this turn. The player uses another philosopher or ends the turn.
4. On a later turn, Hume's Causal Fracture consumes Kant's Guard. It removes no influence this time. Another ready philosopher can follow up.

This creates a tactical rhythm of building a position, challenging its defenses, and deciding when to commit a second thinker. Animations can illustrate a cracked causal chain meeting a golden geometric shield.

## Agreement, conflict and history

Cooperation emerges from effects: Locke builds influence across arenas; Spinoza gathers it into a contested position. Sartre relocates to an empty question; Beauvoir supports a philosopher elsewhere. Schopenhauer makes repeated argument costly; Bataille spends a surplus in a single burst.

For an educational layer, a voluntary “Why these two?” panel can explain relationships using the existing storybook sources. Distinguish direct criticism, influence, broad affinity and an imagined cross-century encounter. In particular, combo animations are invented gameplay, not evidence that two thinkers agreed.

No automatic bonus for every vaguely related pair in the first version: the rules should remain learnable without memorizing a hidden relationship matrix.

## Presentation

- Keep the existing painted portraits, holographic cards and five rarities.
- A three-column table makes the debate arenas readable. Your cards sit below each question; the opponent's sit above it. Influence and the current lead appear beside the question.
- Your six cards begin in a visible hand. The opponent's unplayed choices are hidden until deployed; a local two-person mode needs a handoff screen. A simpler teaching mode can expose both hands explicitly.
- Show legal target highlights, exact costs, projected influence changes and a plain-language explanation before confirmation.
- Animate a selected card forward; draw each signature as a short visual metaphor. Respect reduced-motion settings and keep an effects toggle.
- Explain the last action in the log: “Hume challenged Reality. Kant's Guard absorbed the loss.” Every result must be reproducible from the rules.
- Offer a guided first match, an AI opponent, same-device play and later private online rooms. These are planned modes, not existing features.

## Framework choice

**boardgame.io** is the best fit for the rules layer. Its official documentation describes turn/phase management, move validation hooks, multiplayer synchronization, bots, logs and support for a vanilla JavaScript interface. The existing card presentation can remain HTML/CSS rather than being rebuilt in a game engine.

Sources checked for this design:

- https://boardgame.io/
- https://github.com/boardgameio/boardgame.io
- https://boardgame.io/documentation/turn-order.html
- https://github.com/boardgameio/boardgame.io/blob/main/docs/documentation/multiplayer.md

Use a pinned, tested release. Model team selection, play and completion as phases; model deploy, move, argue, activate and pass as legal moves. Keep rule functions separate from animation. The server must enforce costs, targets and hidden information for online play. Online rooms still require hosting and operational setup; a framework does not provide a deployed service automatically.

Rune is another documented multiplayer option, particularly when targeting its distribution platform: https://developers.rune.ai/docs/quick-start . For this existing standalone browser collection, boardgame.io is the more direct architectural fit.

## Balance and validation before release

This is a playable-on-paper draft, not a claim of competitive balance. Test resource caps, action exhaustion, Guard consumption, alternating initiative, target legality, all 23 signatures and end-of-round scoring. Simulate varied teams and mirror matches to examine first-player advantage and runaway leads. Specifically watch Bruno's spread, Kant's repeatable Guard, Marx's two-arena payoff and Bataille's stored-resource burst. Tune numbers after those observations.

A bot should select from legal moves and evaluate expected prestige, threatened arenas, influence margins and Insight efficiency. A language model is unnecessary for adjudicating rules. AI-generated dialogue, if added later, should remain optional flavor.
