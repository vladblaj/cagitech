The Motif Index

A symbol is not a word — it does not hold still. The philosophical egg, the sealed glass vessel in which the alchemical work is done, is cracked open into a tavern on the back of Bosch's Tree-Man around 1500; it is struck with a fiery sword in Maier's eighth emblem in 1617; and it turns up in 1937 in the petrified hand of Dalí's Narcissus, split, with a flower growing out of it. Nobody coordinated this. Choose a motif to follow it forward and watch what each age needed it to mean.

The Seven Currents

Seven distinct reasons a picture stops being readable. They are not schools or styles — a Bosch and a Tibetan wheel have nothing in common formally — but systems of knowledge, each of which produced images that made complete sense inside it and very little outside it.

The Cartography of Strangeness

A map of encoded painting, 1400–1970

Every picture on this map was built to be read, and every one of them has stopped being fully readable. That is the subject. These are not paintings that are strange because their makers were strange; they are strange because they were precision instruments for a system of knowledge that has since collapsed — a cosmology, a laboratory practice, a scheme of correspondences, a private religion — and we are looking at the instrument without the manual.

The conventional way to arrange such pictures is by date or by school. That tells you almost nothing about why they resist you. So this map uses a second axis instead: how far the image was ever intended to travel. Some of these works were public sermons, legible at a glance to an illiterate congregation. Others were locked boxes made for one patron, one circle, one initiate — or, at the extreme, for nobody at all.

The two axes
Horizontal — timeLinear years, 1400 to 1970. Scroll or pinch to zoom, drag to pan. The clustering is real: certain decades produce a burst of hermetic imagery, and those decades are almost always ones in which an old order of knowledge is visibly breaking.
Vertical — depth of encodingAt the bottom, public allegory: a Wheel of Life painted at a monastery gate to teach farmers. At the top, private language: Wölfli's 25,000 pages, or Hilma af Klint's temple paintings sealed for twenty years after her death. The middle is the interesting country — courtly ciphers, humanist puzzles, images that wanted to be understood by roughly nine people.
What the marks mean
ColourThe current a work belongs to — alchemical, Neoplatonic, eschatological, folk, non-European cosmological, occult-modern, visionary. The inner disc is sampled from the work's own palette.
SizeSymbolic density: how many separately decodable signs are packed into the frame. Bosch is large. De Chirico is small and hits harder.
ThreadsSelect a motif in the rail above — the egg, the black sun, the sphinx, the severed head — and the map draws every work that carries it. This is the argument of the whole thing: these images are not isolated eccentricities but a transmission network running for six hundred years.

On credulity

A map like this attracts nonsense, so a rule was applied throughout: scholarly consensus, live scholarly dispute, and popular fantasy are labelled as such and never blended. Where a famous occult reading is simply false — the Priory of Sion behind Poussin's tomb, the endless "solutions" to the Voynich — the entry says so and says why. The real history is stranger than the invented one, and it has the advantage of being true.
