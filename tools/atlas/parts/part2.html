Lost in 1940, never recovered

Why the Carpathians decide everything. Romania is the only country in Europe built around a mountain range rather than against one. The arc encloses Transylvania like a wall with gates; the lands outside it — Wallachia on the Danube plain, Moldavia on the eastern approaches — face open ground and every army that ever crossed it. For most of recorded history the inside and the outside belonged to different empires. That single fact explains more of what follows than any king does.

One thread, two thousand years

The gold that keeps leaving

If you want a single story that runs the whole length of this map, it is not a dynasty or a language. It is bullion — repeatedly accumulated here, and repeatedly removed.

AD 106

Trajan empties Dacia

Trajan's own physician, Crito, recorded a haul so large — millions of pounds of gold — that modern historians read it as a copying error and revise it down to roughly 165 tonnes of gold and 331 of silver. Even revised, it paid for 123 consecutive days of games, a new forum, and a column.

Rome then mined Alburnus Maior for 166 years and took out an estimated 500 tonnes more.

c. 375 · 1799

Two hoards, both abroad

The Pietroasele treasure — Gothic, 22 gold pieces — was found in 1837, sold to a stonemason, recovered, then stolen from the National Museum in 1875 and partly hammered flat.

The 23 gold vessels of Sânnicolau Mare, 9.9 kg, carry an inscription in a runiform script nobody has convincingly read. Found in the Banat in 1799; in Vienna ever since.

1916

The train to Moscow

With the front collapsing, the government sent the national reserve to its Russian ally for safekeeping: 91.5 tonnes of fine gold in 1,738 sealed crates, seventeen wagons, out of Iași on 16 December 1916. A second shipment in 1917 carried the Academy's archives, the crown jewels and the Pietroasele gold.

Lenin's government repudiated the deposit in January 1918. Some artefacts returned in 1935 and 1956. Not one gram of the gold ever has.

1995–2021

The one that stayed

A plan to reopen Roșia Montană — the Roman gold mountain — as an open-cast cyanide operation would have destroyed four peaks and moved a village. It produced the largest street protests since 1989.

The project was stopped; the state won the resulting $4.4bn arbitration in 2024. In 2021 UNESCO listed the Roman galleries. The gold is still in the mountain — which, on this timeline, is the anomaly.

Method & caveats

How these maps were drawn

Projection

Lambert conformal conic, standard parallels 44°N and 48°N, central meridian 25°E. Frame: 18.4°–31.6°E, 42.6°–49.2°N. Identical on every plate, which is the whole point — shapes are directly comparable.

Coastlines & regions

Natural Earth 1:10m administrative units and rivers (public domain). Historical regions are built by grouping modern units, then clipping against real river geometry — Bessarabia and Bukovina are cut on the actual Dniester, not a straight line.

Checked against the record

Where a treaty states an area, the drawn polygon was measured against it. Northern Transylvania comes out at 43,423 km² against the Second Vienna Award's 43,104. Bessarabia: 44,804 against ~44,422. Transnistria 1941: 38,358 against ~39,700.

What is approximate

Burebista's realm is a sphere of influence, not a border, and is drawn deliberately soft. Roman Dacia's limes, the 1940 award line and southern Bessarabia are hand-digitised. Medieval frontiers were zones, not lines; treat every pre-1850 edge as an argument, not a measurement.
